An unsupervised trash-qubit quantum autoencoder for dijet anomaly detection,
measured against classical autoencoders, PCA, and a single-variable cut, on the
standard **LHC Olympics 2020 R&D** benchmark.

The point of the project is the comparison, not the quantum model: the question
asked is whether a few-dozen-parameter variational circuit buys anything over
classical baselines given the same inputs, the same training loop, and the same
evaluation. The answer is reported whichever way it comes out.

The rejection tests are run on **log(1/ε_B)**, not 1/ε_B. The rejection is a ratio and is strongly right-skewed across seeds, so a t-test on the raw value violates its normality assumption and lets one large seed dominate. Differences of logs are near-symmetric; the column shows the exponentiated mean difference, i.e. a multiplicative factor (x1.00 = no difference), with ±1 s.e. exponentiated as the bracket.

Two-sided paired t-tests across 15 seeds. Within a seed every model sees exactly the same events, so pairing removes the split-to-split component and is more sensitive than comparing independent mean ± s.d. summaries.

**What the quoted ± measures.** It is the spread of the whole training run across seeds — weight initialisation plus which background events landed in the train/validation/test split. It is *not* a test-set statistical uncertainty, and the seeds are **not independent experiments**: each seed's test set contains 880,000 of the same 1,000,000 background events (88% of the pool) and all 100,000 signal events, so the test sets overlap heavily and the quoted spread understates what independent replication would show.

**Test-set uncertainty, separately.** At these sample sizes (100,000 signal, 880,000 background) the Hanley–McNeil standard error on a single AUC is ~0.0010, far smaller than the observed across-seed spread. Almost all of the variation reported here is therefore training variability, not finite-test-set noise.

With 15 seeds the power is low: *not significant* means **not resolved by this study**, not *identical*.

### What the numbers say

Two factual corrections that hold regardless of interpretation:

* The earlier claim that the dense AE's spread came "from initialisation alone"
  was wrong. Each seed re-initialises every model **and** re-draws the
  train/validation/test split, so the two effects are not separated by this
  design. In the current run the dense AE spans 0.4285 → 0.7267 across seeds
  (range 0.298). Because PCA and the m_j1 cut see the *same* resampled splits
  and vary by only 0.014 and 0.0004 respectively, the variance is attributable
  to training rather than to the data split — but the two sources are not
  individually measured here.
* A third source of variation is **not** in that spread at all, and it is
  large for the classical autoencoders: their per-seed AUC is not reproducible
  across machines. Re-running the identical configuration and seeds on a
  different CPU, Python and torch moves `ae_matched` by up to 0.157, which
  exceeds its across-seed s.d.; perturbing the training data by one part in
  10¹⁵ and retraining moves it by up to 0.169. The two quantum models, PCA
  and the m_j1 cut were identical across those same machines to every decimal
  place quoted here. So the ± on the five classical rows understates their
  true variability, and their per-seed numbers should not be read as
  properties of the model. See `tools/diagnose_training_chaos.py`,
  `tools/verify_reproduction.py`, and Limitation 12.
* The m_JJ sculpting result is unchanged in direction, with a looser bound
  than previously stated: every learned score gives |ρ(score, m_JJ)| ≤ 0.034
  on background against 0.084 for the m_j1 cut, so holding m_JJ out of the
  inputs worked as intended. This is a property of the decorrelated
  substructure inputs, not of the quantum model — the classical baselines
  achieve it equally.

Headline metrics are ROC-AUC and background rejection 1/ε_B at fixed signal
efficiency ε_S. Both are independent of the signal fraction mixed into the test
set. Training convergence and wall-clock time are diagnostics, not results.

## Finding: ZZFeatureMap cannot compress this data, for any ansatz

The obvious QML choice for encoding — Qiskit's `ZZFeatureMap` — provably cannot
**compress** this data in a trash-qubit autoencoder, and the reason is
structural rather than a matter of tuning.

To be precise about the scope of that claim: what is proved below is a ceiling
on **compression**, not on anomaly-detection performance. The two are separate
questions and only the first is settled here.

> **⚠️ WITHDRAWN PENDING REVIEW.** This section previously continued with an
> empirical comparison between the ZZFeatureMap and RY models, and an argument
> that detection performance is decoupled from compression. That text was
> written against an earlier run and is **not supported by the current
> numbers**. It has been removed rather than restated, and no replacement
> empirical claim is made here in either direction. The generated tables in
> [Results](#results) are the current record; read them directly.

Everything below this notice is mathematics about the encoding and is
unaffected by which run produced the empirical numbers.

`ZZFeatureMap` applies a Hadamard to every qubit and then **only phase gates**.
Every amplitude therefore has modulus 2^(−n/2) for *every* input; distinct
events differ only in phase, are close to mutually orthogonal, and span the full
Hilbert space.

This admits an exact, ansatz-independent bound. A trash-qubit autoencoder
applies one fixed unitary *U* and asks for the trash register to read |0…0⟩,
which means mapping states into a 2^n_latent-dimensional subspace. Writing
ρ = 𝔼ₓ|ψ(x)⟩⟨ψ(x)| for the average encoded state,

```
E_x[ P(trash = |0..0>) ]  =  tr( rho · U† Π U )
max_U E_x[ P(trash = |0..0>) ]  =  sum of the k largest eigenvalues of rho
```

with k = 2^n_latent, by the Ky Fan maximum principle (R. Bhatia, *Matrix
Analysis*, Springer GTM 169, 1997, Ch. II — Problem II.6.15 and the surrounding
discussion of Ky Fan's extremal characterisation of eigenvalue sums).
This is a ceiling no ansatz, depth, or training budget can exceed. For a
maximally mixed ρ it equals k/2ⁿ — exactly the random-guess probability.

`src/encoding_analysis.py` computes it. Both encodings are trained and reported
side by side so the failure is visible rather than quietly omitted.

This is why the model uses **RY angle encoding** (data in amplitudes) rather
than `ZZFeatureMap` (data in phases).

## Data

[LHC Olympics 2020 R&D dataset](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.6466204)
(Kasieczka, Nachman & Shih), Zenodo record 6466204 v5, CC-BY 4.0.

* 1 M QCD dijet background + 100 k signal events
* Signal: W′ → XY, X→qq, Y→qq, with m_W′ = 3.5 TeV, m_X = 500 GeV, m_Y = 100 GeV
* Pythia 8 + Delphes 3.4.1, no pileup or MPI, single fat-jet trigger p_T > 1.2 TeV

Only the **74 MB high-level feature file** (`events_anomalydetection_v2.features.h5`)
is used — jets already clustered anti-k_T R=1 by the dataset authors. The 2.9 GB
raw hadron-level file is **not** needed, which is what makes this run on a free
Colab tier. `src/data.py` downloads it and verifies the md5.

### Inputs

Six features: `mj1`, `Δmj`, `τ21^j1`, `τ21^j2`, `τ32^j1`, `τ32^j2`,
with jets ordered by **mass** (j1 = lighter), so Δmj ≥ 0 by construction.

**m_JJ is deliberately excluded from the inputs.** It is the resonance variable
a real search bump-hunts in, so an anomaly score that depends on it distorts the
spectrum the search relies on. It is retained solely as a sculpting diagnostic —
the reported ρ(score, m_JJ) is the Spearman correlation on background events.
This follows the convention of the ANODE / CATHODE / CWoLa-hunting work on this
dataset.

Features are mapped to [0, π] by a `QuantileTransformer` fit on the **background
training split only**. Quantile rather than min-max because m_j1 has a long tail
that min-max would crush into a narrow angular range. The transform is monotone
per feature, so it cannot change the AUC of any single-feature baseline.

## Model

Trash-qubit quantum autoencoder ([Romero, Olson & Aspuru-Guzik,
arXiv:1612.02806](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.02806); applied to HEP by
[Ngairangbam, Spannowsky & Takeuchi, PRD 105, 095004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04958)):

```
|x> --[ RY angle encoding ]--[ RealAmplitudes(theta), reps ]--  4 latent qubits (kept)
                                                                2 trash qubits (measured)
```

* **6 qubits**, 4 latent + 2 trash; `n_qubits × (reps + 1)` trainable parameters
* Loss `L = 1 − P(trash = |00⟩)`, averaged over **background only**
* The same quantity per event is the anomaly score at test time
* No SWAP test: measuring the trash register directly is equivalent to the
  SWAP-test fidelity against a fresh |00⟩ reference and costs 3 fewer qubits.
  This is the standard simplification and is what Ngairangbam et al. use.

**Ansatz depth is not fixed a priori.** It is selected from `{1, 3, 5, 7, 9}` by
**background validation loss** — no labels, no signal events, no test data.
That is model selection an unsupervised practitioner can legitimately do;
selecting depth by test AUC would be tuning on the evaluation set. The selected
depth and the full selection scan are recorded in `results/metrics.json`.

One caveat worth stating: validation compression loss is a *proxy* for anomaly
detection, not the same thing. A model that compresses background better is not
guaranteed to separate signal better — it could in principle compress signal
well too. The criterion is defensible because it is label-free, not because it
is optimal.

This is no longer only a hedge: the generated block pairs, for every model and
seed, the best background validation loss against the test AUC, together with
the rank correlation between them. Read that table before treating a lower
validation loss as evidence of a better detector.

### Why PennyLane trains it and Qiskit specifies it

The cost model that matters here is the **parameter-shift** rule: 2 circuit
evaluations per parameter per step. At a few dozen parameters and 10⁵ training
events that is ~10⁶ circuit executions per epoch, which is not viable on a free
Colab CPU.

To be accurate about Qiskit: parameter-shift is *not* Qiskit's only option on a
simulator. `ReverseEstimatorGradient` is an adjoint-style method that computes
exact gradients far more cheaply, and had this study needed to stay inside
Qiskit that would have been the sensible route. Parameter-shift is quoted as
the cost model because it is the path that carries over to hardware, where
adjoint methods are unavailable — you cannot backpropagate through a physical
device. Any statement here about scaling to real quantum hardware is therefore
governed by the parameter-shift cost, not the simulator cost.

PennyLane's `default.qubit` with `diff_method="backprop"` differentiates through
the statevector simulation directly, giving the *same* exact noiseless gradients
in seconds per epoch.

The Qiskit circuit remains the specification, and `verify_against_qiskit()`
asserts that the two implementations produce the **same statevector to ~10⁻¹⁶**
(accounting for Qiskit's little-endian vs PennyLane's big-endian convention).
That check runs at the start of every study, for both encodings.

## Baselines

Seven, deliberately, all on identical inputs and the same training loop
(`src/train.py`) with the same optimiser, schedule, batching and early stopping.
Each model family gets its own learning rate, selected on background validation
loss alone (see Model), because a single shared rate silently imposes one
family's operating point on the others:

| baseline | why it is here |
|---|---|
| Tied-weight AE, **parameter-matched** (48 par.) | exact match to the QAE's parameter count |
| Untied AE, 32 par. (latent 2) | brackets the QAE's 48 from below, no tying |
| Untied AE, 45 par. (latent 3) | brackets it from below, no tying |
| Untied AE, 58 par. (latent 4) | brackets it from above, no tying; same latent as the QAE |
| Dense AE, 6-16-4-16-6 (362 par.) | what a practitioner would actually build |
| PCA, 4 components | linear reconstruction error, hard to argue with |
| **Cut on m_j1 alone** | the control that matters most |

The matched AE is **built to order at run time**, because the QAE's parameter
count is `n_qubits × (reps + 1)` for whatever depth gets selected, not a fixed
number. `matched_ae()` searches tied-AE configurations (latent size, optional
output affine, latent bias, latent gain) for one with *exactly* the target
count, preferring the latent dimension closest to the quantum model's, and
raises rather than silently reporting an unmatched "matched" baseline.

### The exact match forces weight tying, and that favours the QAE

This needs stating because it is not visible from the table. A conventional
**untied** autoencoder `6 → k → 6` with biases has `2·6·k + k + 6` parameters:

| latent k | untied parameters |
|---|---:|
| 2 | 32 |
| 3 | 45 |
| 4 | **58** |

The untied counts are `2·6·k + k + 6`, i.e. **19, 32, 45, 58, 71, 84** for
k = 1…6. The QAE's parameter count is whatever `6·(reps+1)` the selected depth
gives — 48 in the current run — and **48 is not in that list.** Neither was the
36 of an earlier run. The exact-count match is therefore reachable only by
tying the encoder and decoder weights (`x̂ = tanh(xWᵀ)W`), which halves the
effective capacity at fixed parameter count. Weight tying is a real handicap,
imposed on the classical side purely to satisfy the matching requirement, so it
**runs in the QAE's favour**. An untied 6→4→6 autoencoder — the thing a
practitioner would actually write — has 58 parameters and cannot appear in an
exactly-parameter-matched comparison at all.

Three untied autoencoders at **32**, **45** and **58** parameters are therefore
reported alongside, bracketing the QAE's 48 from below (32, 45) and above (58),
each trained through the same loop with its own selected learning rate. Where
the QAE falls within that bracket is the relevant comparison; the
parameter-matched row cannot settle it, because at this depth that row has no
bottleneck (below).

A second, related distortion appears at larger ansatz depths: for a reps=7 or
reps=9 QAE the exact-match target (48 or 60 parameters) is only reachable at
latent 6 — that is, with **no bottleneck at all** for a 6-dimensional input.
Exact parameter matching and equal compression cannot both be held; this study
holds the parameter count fixed and `run_study.py` prints a warning whenever
the matched model's latent dimension departs from the QAE's.

Reporting only the matched AE would look like handicapping the classical side;
reporting only the dense AE would be unfair to the QAE. Both are given.

The m_j1 control is the one to look at first. Autoencoders on jet features are
known to score largely as a proxy for jet mass, so **if the QAE does not beat a
plain threshold on m_j1, there is no result here.**

**Outcome of that test in the current run.** The numbers that were quoted here
were from an earlier, smaller run and did not survive it; rather than restate
them, they are removed. The two comparisons to read are *QAE vs. a plain cut on
m_J1* in the ROC-AUC table and in the rejection table of the generated block
above, together with their *survives correction?* column at the corrected
threshold stated there. Read those rows directly. No conclusion is drawn here.

**On "matched parameter count":** the QAE keeps 4 of its 6 qubits. The
classical baselines do **not** all keep 4 of 6 dimensions. PCA and the dense AE
use latent 4, the untied models use latent 2, 3 and 4 (32, 45 and 58
parameters), and the parameter-matched model uses whatever latent the exact
count forces — currently **6, i.e. no bottleneck at all** (see
[the exact-match discussion](#the-exact-match-forces-weight-tying-and-that-favours-the-qae)
and the footnote under the results table). Matching the parameter count and
matching the retained degrees of freedom are different constraints and cannot
both be imposed here. Neither matches Hilbert-space dimension (4 qubits span 16
complex dimensions). No single notion of "same size" applies across the two
model families, which is precisely why several baselines are reported instead
of one.

## Limitations

Stated plainly, because they bound what the numbers mean:

1. **The training set is pure background, and it was built using the labels.**
   `make_splits` selects `label == 0` events to train on. That is idealised
   anomaly detection: a real search trains on data that already contains
   whatever signal is present, and an autoencoder trained on contaminated
   background learns to reconstruct the signal too, which erodes exactly the
   reconstruction error the method relies on. Performance under contamination
   is not measured here at any signal fraction, so none of these numbers
   transfer directly to a real search.
2. **Noiseless statevector simulation, infinite shots.** No hardware noise, no
   shot noise, no decoherence, no connectivity constraints. These results say
   nothing about performance on real quantum hardware.
3. **6 qubits, 6 features.** A small slice of the available information.
   Published LHCO analyses use far more, including full jet constituents.
4. **Subsampled training set** — 100 k of the 1 M background events by default.
   All remaining background goes to the test set to maximise the statistics
   available for rejection at fixed efficiency.
5. **Lightly tuned, and the selections are not resolved.** Two things are now
   selected, both on background validation loss only: ansatz depth (from
   `{1, 3, 5, 7, 9}`) and learning rate per model family (from the
   `--lr-candidates` grid). Latent size, batch size, ansatz family and the
   classical architectures remain fixed a priori. Selection existing does not
   make this well-tuned: the selections are a single coordinate pass (depth,
   then learning rate) rather than a joint search, and the generated
   configuration block flags every case where a selected value sits at the edge
   of its grid — where that flag is set, the value is the boundary of the
   search, not a resolved optimum. Read the comparison as "lightly-tuned models
   under matched conditions", not as best-versus-best.
6. **The input scaling is a real choice, not a neutral one.** A
   `QuantileTransformer` fit on background makes the background marginals
   uniform, which suits angle encoding. It is applied identically to every
   model and fit only on training background, and being monotone per feature it
   cannot change any single-feature baseline's AUC — but a different scaler
   would give different absolute numbers.
7. **Not comparable to the official LHCO leaderboard.** That challenge scored
   *black-box* bump hunts by discovery significance, mostly with weakly
   supervised methods. This is unsupervised anomaly detection on the R&D
   dataset — the same data, a different task. Comparisons should be made against
   published unsupervised autoencoder numbers, not the challenge results.
8. **Unsupervised autoencoders are not state of the art on LHCO.** Weakly
   supervised approaches substantially outperform them. No SOTA claim is made.
9. **Detector realism is whatever Delphes provides.** No pileup, no MPI, no
   systematic uncertainties.
10. **Signal-model coverage.** Training and model selection use one signal
   model at one mass point (W' 3.5 TeV, X 500 GeV, Y 100 GeV, two-prong). A
   second, never-trained-on signal (the LHCO three-prong W' -> XY, X,Y -> qqq
   sample) is scored as a generalisation test and reported above. Two signal
   models at one mass point is still narrow: no scan over resonance mass,
   prong multiplicity beyond these two, or non-resonant topologies is
   attempted, so signal-model independence is probed, not demonstrated.
   The generated table reports both signals side by side. The models are not
   ranked identically on the two, which is itself the point: a score's
   performance here is signal-dependent, so a single-signal number should not
   be read as a general anomaly-detection capability.
11. Ngairangbam et al. report a QAE outperforming a classical AE, but on a
   *different* dataset (t̄t background, heavy Higgs signal) with different
   inputs. Their result does not transfer to this benchmark and is not evidence
   for the numbers here.
12. **The classical autoencoder rows are not reproducible at the precision
   they are quoted.** Their optimisation is chaotic at the selected learning
   rates: perturbing the training data by one part in 10¹⁵ and retraining from
   the same seed moves `ae_matched`'s AUC by up to 0.169, and re-running the
   identical configuration and seeds on a different machine moves it by up to
   0.157 — larger, for `ae_matched` and the three untied AEs, than their
   across-seed s.d. The quoted ± therefore understates their variability,
   because it measures seeds and not this. The learning rates were selected on
   a 25-epoch budget over 25 k events, where they looked stable; over 600
   epochs on 100 k events `ae_matched` at lr=0.1 shows validation-loss
   excursions to 2.7 from a baseline near 0.03. Any comparison against these
   baselines whose effect size is smaller than their platform spread is not
   resolved by this study. `qae_ry`, `qae_zz`, `pca` and `mj1_only` were
   identical across the same machines to ~1 ULP. Run
   `tools/diagnose_training_chaos.py` and `tools/verify_reproduction.py` to
   see both halves.

## Reproducing

### What reproduces, and what does not

Not everything here reproduces, and the split is sharp enough to state before
any instructions. Re-running the identical configuration and seeds on a
different machine (different CPU vendor, Python 3.12 vs 3.13, torch 2.14 CPU
vs 2.11 CUDA):

* `qae_ry`, `qae_zz`, `pca` and `mj1_only` agreed to **1 unit in the last place
  of a float64** — closer than any number quoted in this README resolves.
* the five classical autoencoders did **not**, moving by up to 0.157 in AUC.

`pca` and `mj1_only` involve no gradient training at all, so their agreement to
~1 ULP also establishes that the splits, the quantile transform and the metric
code are bit-identical across the two machines. That is what makes the
classical drift attributable to the optimisation rather than to data handling:
the models that only *read* the data agree exactly, and the models that
*descend* on it do not. See Limitation 12.

```bash
python tools/verify_reproduction.py              # re-run seeds 0-2, diff per model
python tools/diagnose_training_chaos.py          # why five models drift
```

### GPU

A GPU helps substantially and the reported numbers were produced on one: a
Tesla T4 ran the quantum models at **1.69 s/epoch** against **12.2 s/epoch** on
a Ryzen 5 5600H laptop CPU, turning a 6.5 h study into about one hour. The
work is memory-bandwidth-bound rather than arithmetic-bound — thread scaling
on the laptop saturates at 3 of 6 cores, and running seeds in parallel
processes was measured to be 23–28% *slower* in aggregate than running them one
after another (4 and 6 workers respectively, one thread each, against one
6-thread process) — which is exactly the case a GPU's memory bandwidth
addresses. Only the
quantum models are moved to the device; the classical baselines train in
0.03 s each and stay on the CPU.
